AI Advisory for Local Service-Based Small Businesses

AI can help your business.
It also needs boundaries.

You may already be using ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or another AI tool to write emails, put together marketing content, answer questions, organize information, or get through the administrative work that piles up when you're busy actually running the business.

The question isn't whether your business should use AI. It's knowing what AI should have access to, where your information goes, and where the boundaries need to be.

I help local service businesses build AI workflows that fit the way they already work.

You don't need an enterprise AI strategy.

You need to know what makes sense for your business.

A plumber, HVAC company, salon, photographer, landscaper, repair shop, cleaning company, or contractor does not need a 70-page AI governance plan. You probably need answers to much more practical questions:

  • Which AI tools actually make sense for what we do?
  • What can employees safely put into them?
  • What customer or business information should stay out?
  • Should anything run locally instead of in the cloud?
  • Who should have access?
  • Where should passwords and API keys live?
  • Should AI be allowed to take actions, or only make recommendations?
  • Where does a human need to approve something?
  • What happens to the information we hand these tools?
  • Are we paying for five different AI tools that solve the same problem?

Those are architecture questions. Answering them before you connect everything together is a lot easier than untangling it afterward.

Start with the business. Then choose the AI.

There's no AI stack that's right for every small business, so I start with what you actually do.

What takes your time? What information moves through the business? What do employees need access to? Where are the repetitive tasks? What contains customer information? What absolutely cannot break?

Then we decide where AI is useful.

That may mean cloud AI.
It may mean local AI.
It may mean automation.
It may mean keeping a human approval step in the middle.

And sometimes the right answer is simply:

AI doesn't need access to that.

What an AI Setup Review looks at

Your current AI use

We look at the AI tools already being used in the business, including the unofficial ones. A policy that says "don't use AI" doesn't tell you what somebody pasted into ChatGPT at 4:30 yesterday afternoon.

Your information

We sort the kinds of information your business handles, separating ordinary working information from the things that deserve more protection. Customer records. Employee records. Contracts. Financials. Credentials. Internal know-how. Not everything carries the same risk.

Cloud vs. local

Cloud AI isn't automatically unsafe. Local AI isn't automatically private. We look at what belongs where, based on the information involved and the controls you actually have.

Access and security

Who can use the AI? What can it read? What can it change? Where are its credentials stored? What happens when an employee leaves? A useful AI system needs boundaries around both information and authority.

Human approval

There's a big difference between "draft a response to this customer" and "send this response to the customer." We map where AI can work on its own and where a person stays in the loop.

The whole pile

One piece of information may be harmless. Customer lists, pricing, vendors, schedules, margins, complaints, renewal dates, procedures, and upcoming work collected together can say quite a lot about a business. We look at the pile, not just each piece.

Local AI, when it makes sense

Local AI can be a good fit when your business needs tighter control over internal knowledge, or when you'd rather not send certain information to an outside provider as a matter of routine.

But installing a model on a computer doesn't make it secure.

A properly designed local setup may need authentication, device encryption, protected credentials, limited file access, network controls, backups, logging, and human approval boundaries.

Think less "I downloaded an AI model."
Think more "Who has the keys, and what do those keys open?"
